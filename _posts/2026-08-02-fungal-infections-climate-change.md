*The Last of Us* imagined a mutated Cordyceps fungus turning people into "the infected." It made for gripping television, and it sent a lot of people searching "can fungi actually infect human brains?"

![A real Ophiocordyceps fungus fruiting from an infected ant on a moss-covered branch](/field-notes/assets/img/Ophiocordyceps-macro.png)

The zombie apocalypse part is fiction. But the show accidentally stumbled onto something real: fungi are becoming a bigger threat to humans, and climate change is the reason why.

## Why the Zombie Scenario Doesn't Hold Up

*Ophiocordyceps unilateralis*, the real "zombie ant fungus," genuinely hijacks the mind and motor functions of specific ant species before killing the host and releasing spores. But it's highly specialized. A strain that infects one ant species can't even infect a different species of ant, let alone jump to humans.

![Side-by-side comparison of how fungal infections spread from the environment versus how viral infections spread person to person](/field-notes/assets/img/Spore-vs-Viral-Spread.png)

Two barriers make that leap especially unlikely. Fungi rarely spread person to person the way viruses do, since they're mostly acquired from the environment through spores or a wound. And most fungal species simply can't survive above 98.6°F, human body temperature acts as a built in wall that has protected us for millions of years.

That second barrier is the one now under real pressure.

## The Wall Is Cracking

Fungi in the wild are constantly exposed to rising heat from heatwaves and warming climates. Lab studies show this pressure can push fungal evolution surprisingly fast. Growing a dangerous fungal pathogen at human body temperature instead of a cooler baseline caused a fivefold jump in its mutation rate. A separate insect pathogen evolved the ability to grow five degrees Celsius hotter than its previous limit in just four months.

![Chart showing fungal growth curves shifting closer to the 98.6°F human body temperature barrier as fungi adapt to a warming climate](/field-notes/assets/img/Wall-Is-Cracking.png)

As climate change keeps selecting for heat tolerant fungal strains, some are edging closer to surviving inside us, and there's early evidence this can make them more aggressive and harder to treat with existing drugs.

*Candida auris* is the case researchers point to most. Unknown to medicine before 2009, it was first isolated from a patient in Japan and spread to hospitals in more than 30 countries within a decade. Genomic evidence suggests it gained tolerance for human body temperature relatively recently, exactly the pattern climate driven adaptation would produce.

## Why We Can't Treat Fungi Like Bacteria

This is the part that makes fungal disease a uniquely hard problem, and it has nothing to do with climate.

![Comparison of a bacterial cell, a fungal cell, and a human cell, showing the fungal and human cells share structural similarities that bacteria don't](/field-notes/assets/img/Bacteria-Fungi-Human-Cell.png)

Bacteria and human cells are fundamentally different at the molecular level, so antibiotics can target bacterial structures our own cells simply don't have. Fungi don't give us that advantage. Fungal cells are eukaryotic, just like ours, so the internal machinery a drug might target (ribosomes, membranes, cell division proteins) looks structurally similar to our own. A compound strong enough to kill the fungus often risks damaging human cells too.

That's the core reason antifungal drug development has lagged so far behind antibiotics and antivirals, and why only a handful of major antifungal drug classes exist today.

## It's Not Just Severe Cases Anymore

Invasive, hospital acquired infections get the headlines. But the quieter trend is arguably more relevant to most people: common, mild fungal infections, persistent ringworm, stubborn athlete's foot, recurring skin rashes, are increasingly showing resistance to standard treatments. CDC data shows US deaths from fungal infections rose from a few hundred a year around 1970 to over 7,000 by 2021, a shift researchers partly link to the same warming trend.

## The Real Takeaway

*The Last of Us* got the mechanism wrong but the underlying anxiety right. We're not facing a fungal zombie outbreak. We're facing a slower, structural problem: a defense that worked for millions of years is weakening, right as our drug toolbox remains stuck at just a few options.

As someone training in microbiology, this is exactly the kind of unglamorous but urgent problem I find compelling, the search for fungal specific drug targets that spare human cells is a genuinely open and important research space.
