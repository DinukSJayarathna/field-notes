When the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant suffered its catastrophic meltdown in 1986, the surrounding area became one of the most radioactive environments on Earth, seemingly destined to stay a lifeless ruin.

In 1991, five years after the disaster, scientists sent remote-controlled robots into the wreckage of Reactor Unit Four to survey the damage. The camera feed showed something no one expected: the interior walls, among the most radioactive surfaces on the planet, were black. Not scorched, not corroded, but colonized. A team from the Kyiv Institute of Microbiology and Virology began investigating, and what they found was a thick, dark fungal growth that appeared to be spreading toward the most radioactive spots in the building rather than away from them.

![Black fungal growth glowing near a radiation source inside Chernobyl's ruined reactor](/field-notes/assets/img/Chernobyl-Fungus.png)

That directional habit has a name: **positive radiotropism**, growth oriented toward a radiation source. It's a different claim from what came next, and it's worth keeping the two separate.

## Extremophile is not the same as radiotrophic

Life surviving radioactive environments isn't new by itself. Extremophiles, organisms adapted to extreme conditions, are found in plenty of hostile settings: boiling vents, acidic lakes, deep permafrost. Tolerating radiation fits into that same broad category.

What researchers eventually proposed for the Chernobyl fungi was a step further than tolerance. About a decade after the initial discovery, radiopharmacologist Ekaterina Dadachova and colleagues at the Albert Einstein College of Medicine tested three of the melanin-rich species collected from the reactor, *Cladosporium sphaerospermum*, *Cryptococcus neoformans*, and *Wangiella dermatitidis*, and found they grew measurably faster when exposed to ionizing radiation than under normal background levels. That result is where the idea of *radiosynthesis* comes from: not just surviving despite the radiation, but possibly growing because of it.

## The pigment doing the heavy lifting

Melanin is a familiar molecule. It's the same pigment responsible for skin, hair, and eye color in humans, well known for absorbing UV radiation and dissipating it as heat, which is part of how it protects skin from sun damage.

In these fungi, melanin is packed densely into the cell wall, dense enough that the organisms appear jet black. The proposed mechanism works in two stages. First, the melanin absorbs incoming ionizing radiation, shielding the rest of the cell from damage that would otherwise be lethal. Second, and far more speculatively, the melanin's structure may allow it to shuttle some of that absorbed energy into an electron-transfer process, potentially feeding it into the fungus's normal metabolism as usable chemical energy.

**Example:** picture a solar panel doing two jobs at once, absorbing intense sunlight so it doesn't damage what's underneath, while simultaneously converting a portion of that same energy into something the system underneath can actually use.

## What has actually been measured, and what has not

This is a case where a genuinely interesting finding has been stretched into a bigger claim than the data fully supports, so it's worth being precise.

What has been observed:
- The 1991 discovery of thick fungal growth on reactor walls, apparently oriented toward radiation sources (positive radiotropism)
- Faster growth of melanin-rich fungal species under elevated ionizing radiation compared to normal background levels, in laboratory testing
- In 2016, NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory sent several fungal species collected from Chernobyl to the International Space Station to observe their behavior under space radiation levels roughly 40 to 80 times higher than on Earth
- A later, separate cultivation experiment aboard the ISS in 2022 found *Cladosporium sphaerospermum* grew modestly faster than an equivalent ground control

What has not been confirmed:
- Actual radiosynthesis, meaning a demonstrated conversion of absorbed radiation into a usable form of chemical energy that drives growth, has not been directly shown
- No study has isolated a biomass or carbon-fixation increase that can be attributed specifically to energy harvested from radiation, rather than to melanin simply shielding the cell well enough that ordinary growth proceeds undisturbed
- The response isn't universal across melanized fungi either. A related black yeast, *Wangiella dermatitidis*, does show enhanced growth under radiation, while a different species, *Cladosporium cladosporioides*, increases melanin production under radiation without a matching growth boost

So the fungus clearly does better with radiation present than an unprotected organism would. Whether that's because it's harvesting the radiation as fuel, or because its melanin shield is simply that effective, is still an open question.

## Why researchers care beyond the novelty

This isn't purely a curiosity. Two applications are being actively explored:

- **Space radiation shielding.** If melanin's radiation-absorbing properties can be reliably engineered and scaled, it's been proposed as a self-regenerating, biological shielding layer for spacecraft and long-duration habitats, an appealing alternative to heavy fixed shielding that has to be launched from Earth.
- **Bioremediation.** Researchers are also investigating whether these fungi, or the melanin they produce, could play a role in managing radioactive waste sites, though this remains an early research direction rather than a proven cleanup method.

## A quick summary

- In 1991, remote-controlled robots discovered thick black fungal growth on the walls of Chernobyl's Reactor Unit Four, apparently oriented toward radiation sources (positive radiotropism)
- The species involved, including ***Cladosporium sphaerospermum***, ***Cryptococcus neoformans***, and ***Wangiella dermatitidis***, are all dense with **melanin**
- **Radiosynthesis** is the hypothesis that melanin absorbs ionizing radiation and channels some of that energy into the fungus's metabolism, similar in concept to photosynthesis
- Enhanced growth under radiation has been observed in lab conditions and aboard the ISS, but the actual energy-conversion mechanism has not been directly proven
- Active research interest centers on space radiation shielding and, more speculatively, bioremediation

The label "radiation-eating fungus" makes a great headline, but the more accurate picture right now is a fungus with an extraordinarily good radiation shield, one that might also be doing something more.
