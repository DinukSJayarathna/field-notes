*The Last of Us* imagined a mutated Cordyceps fungus turning people into "the infected." It made for gripping television, and it sent a lot of people searching whether fungi can actually infect the human brain.

![A real Ophiocordyceps fungus fruiting from an infected ant on a moss-covered branch](/field-notes/assets/img/Ophiocordyceps-macro.png)

The zombie apocalypse part is fiction. But the show accidentally stumbled onto something real: fungi are becoming a bigger threat to humans, and climate change is a large part of why.

## Why the zombie scenario doesn't hold up

*Ophiocordyceps unilateralis*, the real "zombie ant fungus" pictured above, genuinely hijacks the mind and motor functions of specific ant species before killing the host and releasing spores. But the system is highly specialized. A strain that infects one ant species can't even infect a different species of ant, let alone jump to humans.

Two biological barriers make that leap especially unlikely, and they come down to how fungi spread compared to viruses.

![Side-by-side comparison of how fungal infections spread from the environment versus how viral infections spread person to person](/field-notes/assets/img/Spore-vs-Viral-Spread.png)

Fungal infections are mostly acquired from the environment, through spores in soil, damp surfaces, or contact with animals, and they are not typically passed directly from person to person. Viral infections work almost the opposite way, spreading efficiently through respiratory droplets from one infected person to the next. That difference alone rules out an outbreak that spreads through a population the way a virus would.

The second barrier is thermal. Most fungal species simply cannot survive at human body temperature, an internal defense that has protected us for millions of years without us ever having to think about it.

That second barrier is the one now under real pressure.

## The wall is cracking

Fungi in the wild are constantly exposed to rising heat from heatwaves and warming climates. Laboratory studies show this pressure can push fungal evolution surprisingly fast.

![Chart showing fungal growth curves shifting closer to the 98.6°F human body temperature barrier as fungi adapt to a warming climate](/field-notes/assets/img/Wall-Is-Cracking.png)

Growing a dangerous fungal pathogen at human body temperature instead of a cooler baseline has been shown to cause a measurable rise in its mutation rate. Separately, researchers have documented an insect-infecting fungus evolving the ability to grow several degrees Celsius hotter than its previous limit in just a few months in the lab.

As climate change keeps selecting for heat-tolerant fungal strains, some are edging closer to surviving inside us, and there is early evidence this same pressure can make them more aggressive and harder to treat with existing drugs.

***Candida auris*** is the case researchers point to most. Unknown to medicine before 2009, it was first isolated from a patient in Japan and had spread to hospitals in more than thirty countries within a decade. Genomic evidence suggests it gained tolerance for human body temperature relatively recently, exactly the pattern climate-driven adaptation would produce.

## Why we can't treat fungi like bacteria

This is the part that makes fungal disease a uniquely hard problem, and it has nothing to do with climate at all.

![Comparison of a bacterial cell, a fungal cell, and a human cell, showing the fungal and human cells share structural similarities that bacteria don't](/field-notes/assets/img/Bacteria-Fungi-Human-Cell.png)

Bacteria and human cells are fundamentally different at the molecular level. Bacterial cells lack a nucleus and have distinct cell wall chemistry, which gives antibiotics clear targets that simply don't exist in our own cells.

Fungi don't give us that same advantage. Fungal cells are eukaryotic, just like ours, so much of the internal machinery a drug might target, ribosomes, membranes, cell division proteins, looks structurally similar to our own equivalent. A compound strong enough to kill the fungus often risks damaging human cells in the process.

That single fact is the core reason antifungal drug development has lagged so far behind antibiotics and antivirals, and why only a handful of major antifungal drug classes exist today, decades into modern medicine.

## It's not just severe cases anymore

Invasive, hospital-acquired infections get the headlines. But a quieter trend is arguably more relevant to most people: common, mild fungal infections such as persistent ringworm, stubborn athlete's foot, and recurring skin rashes are increasingly showing resistance to standard treatments. CDC data shows deaths from fungal infections in the United States rose from a few hundred a year around 1970 to over 7,000 by 2021, a shift researchers partly link to this same warming trend.

## A quick summary

- Fungal infections spread from the environment, not person to person, which is why an outbreak-style epidemic like the one in *The Last of Us* is not realistic
- Human body temperature has acted as a natural barrier against most fungi for millions of years
- Climate change is measurably pushing fungal strains to adapt to higher temperatures, narrowing that safety margin
- ***Candida auris*** is the clearest real-world example of a fungus that appears to have recently gained tolerance for human body heat
- Fungi are eukaryotic like human cells, unlike bacteria, which is why safe antifungal drugs are so much harder to design
- Rising resistance is now showing up in common, mild infections, not just severe hospital cases

*The Last of Us* got the mechanism wrong but the underlying anxiety right. This isn't a fungal zombie outbreak. It's a slower, structural problem: a defense that has worked for millions of years is weakening, right as our drug toolbox remains stuck at just a handful of options.
